In Georgian England, a clergyman'sdaughter watched her world with a sharpand knowing eye, Jane Austin, who turnedthe quiet drawing room into a stage forrebellion. Her subject was the worldthat ruled women's lives, courtship,class, and money. She publishedanonymously. For a woman, the novel wasone of the few ways to be heard, sofiction became her argument. Socialcriticism wrapped inside comedy andromance. Her irony skewered a marriagemarket that weighed a woman's worth inpounds and prospects, not in love. Andher heroins were no meek angels, nonemore so than Emma, handsome, clever,rich, and gloriously flawed. She gavewomen minds of their own on the page, ifnowhere else. Jane Austin, who with witalone said what her world would not letwomen say, and has stayed in print andbeloved for two centuries.
